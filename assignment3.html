Karen
When I was younger I lived near the German border so I often interacted with both french and german at the same time. This led me to become almost bilingual at a young age but my pears found it odd that I spoke two languages and wanted me to conform to only speaking french. They also thought it was weird that I spoke german as a second language instead of english since it’s more standardized than german. Due to this discrimination I no longer speak german though I still understand it, but I think german is a language that makes it easier to express yourself due to its vocabulary structures, as opposed to french where words aren’t always easy to understand, definitions aren’t always obvious and a lot of words are viewed as too formal to be used in common dialogues. When speaking french I often have to think forward of what I have to say to make myself understood without conveying a wrong meaning. French is a language that is hard to learn, hard to understand and hard to use, self expression is impaired by difficult grammar, therefore I find it hard to identify with this language. I’d rather speak english most of the time, even though it is not my first language. English is a language that tends to be more fluid and easy to use, conjugating verbs is much less confusing, and most importantly english is widely spoken through most countries of the world, allowing more people to interact with each other. French is too strict and clings too much to the academic way of speaking especially when written. A standard version of a language can be useful to new learners, but we shouldn’t cling to it too much and allow for language to be more fluid.
Soso
In regards to my identity, grammar holds a primordial place. I am non-binary, and prefer non-gendered terms to refer to me, which is quite the impossible task when talking in French. French is a heavily gendered language, as are all the romance languages, meaning its entire structure revolves around the division between feminine and masculine. Everything, without exception, is forced into a binary, with masculine as the default, the closest thing there is to a “neutral”. While yes, there are neo-pronouns like “iel”, and the neologism of inclusive writing like “heureux.euse”, these are only the bastardised mix of masculine and feminine. There is no way in French to express a gender identity that is not related to masculinity or femininity, the only other option than these two is a middle ground which excludes anyone who does not associate themselves with the binary. In French, there is no language to talk about me and my non-binary siblings. We are erased from any discussion, because in no way can we be addressed. The French language is exorsexist at its core. The English language, however, does not gender its nouns and adjectives. There are exceptions to this (“son”/”daughter”, “husband”/”wife”, “handsome”/”gorgeous”), but for each word associated to a masculine/feminine gender, there is a gender neutral alternative (“child”, “spouse”, “beautiful”). This is the biggest difference, for me, between when I talk French and English. French has gender inclusive writing, whereas English has gender neutral writing. I cannot express my identity in French the way I can in English, even for something as simple as my pronouns. In French, I use “il/lui”, because it is the closest neutral pronoun we have. However in English, I use “they/them”, because it is truly neutral, there is no gender, no strings, attached.
