When I first saw “grammars of English” I thought this class was going to be a review of english grammar and literacy, kind of like french classes but in english. I believed languages were in constant evolution, we make up new words which sometimes become official, we stop using some words which may be forgotten in time, pronunciation of some words may vary depending on the accent or simply evolve, it doesn’t really make sense to me to stick to some words and claim these are the only right words to use, the reason the language we speak in isn’t a “dead” language like Latin is because language evolves and changes over time. This class has comforted me in this belief and I find it very interesting to learn all the different ways a single language can be used depending on the speakers.
Soso
I was really excited for this class (and still am!) I love learning a language’s grammar. However, I was (pleasantly) surprised to discover that this class is not about teaching us grammar in an academic sense - no rather it is about the cultural and historical importance of grammar and linguistics, and how it is shaped by social and political context. I firmly believe that maintaining the idea of a “correct way” to talk only serves to discriminate against those who do not, or cannot do so. Especially in online spaces, where people are too comfortable correcting others on “bad grammar” unprompted, failing to realise how harmful it can be. The use of non-standard English could be because they speak a regional dialect, or English isn’t their native language, or they have a disability. Multiple neurological disabilities (dyslexia, dysorthographia, autism, …) affect one’s ability to follow standard grammar, thus correcting their “grammar mistakes” often feels humiliating, infantilizing or patronizing. The Grammars of English class helped me confirm my opinion.

When I first learnt about this class, I thought that it would be a class strictly about English grammatical rules, very much like a French grammar class, but I was pleased to see how this class teaches about the different varieties of English. Before starting this class, my opinion on standard language was that defining something as "standard" is often used by elites to show how their way of doing thing is better than how other people's. And it didn't really make sense to me because a language has to evolve with the people that use it thus can't revolve around a small group of people that sets rules for others. And for me, it's irrelevant to point out grammatical mistakes outside of a professional context because as long as we can understand each other, it's not important to speak perfectly. This class strengthened my opinion on standard language by showing the variety of the English language and how using the word "standard" can be used to define others as a lesser or "broken" language.
